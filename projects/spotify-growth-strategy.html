Increasing Spotify users with Emotion Tagging for Content Discovery: A Thought Exercise

By Uma Lamba

So here’s the thing:

Spotify already has some really cool features like prompt-based playlist generation. You can type “💔 breakup” or “🔥 energy” and get a playlist that matches that vibe. I love that, it’s clever, intuitive, and getting better.

But it all starts before the actual listening happens.

You give it a prompt, it gives you a vibe. That’s it.

What if the listening itself became the input?

Instead of asking what mood you want, what if Spotify asked how you actually feel while listening?

Like:

  • “This line hit hard.”
  • “This podcast made me reflective.”
  • “This track made me miss someone.”

That emotional input could become a living signal for:

  • What to recommend next
  • What to group together in your listening history
  • What kind of moments you’re emotionally returning to

My twist? It's not about playlists.

I’m imagining a personal space inside Spotify, not another playlist, but a private emotional corner where users can:

  • Leave voice notes about what they’re feeling
  • Write a quick memory or journal thought while listening
  • Tag something with “rage” or “hopeful” or “grief” or “clarity”

These check-ins, tags, and voice notes could actually double as labeled emotional data, not just UX features.And then that emotional data gets organized with help from ML:

  • by emotion type
  • by genre
  • by energy level
  • even by life phase (if you’re open to that)

Over time, Spotify could use transcript sentiment or acoustic patterns (like tone of the user’s voice note) to enrich or validate emotional tags, creating a quiet but powerful human-in-the-loop annotation system.Kind of like a memory lane, but smarter.

How it could work:

Goal Setting

  • Problem: Can Spotify respond to in-the-moment emotional states?
  • Outcome: Emotional awareness built into the listening experience itself.

Data Collection & Labeling

  • Emotion check-ins, tags, voice notes, journaling — these naturally become labeled data that feed the ML loop.

Annotation Processes

  • User-inputted emotions + NLP/voice signal enrichment from Spotify.
  • Feeds into a semi-supervised system built on both human and machine input.

Model Development

Train models using the following input points:

  • Voice tone and cadence from user audio
  • Sentiment analysis on journal entries and content transcripts
  • Behavioral signals like replays, skips, saves, listens to the end

These models would classify emotional context and cluster listening behavior based on felt experience.

Deployment

The system powers:

  • Emotion-smart playlists
  • An emotion-tagged content feed
  • A “memory space” where users track how different content made them feel over time

Metrics That Matter

  • Emotional Feature Adoption: % of users engaging with emotion check-ins, tags, or voice notes
  • Cross-Modal Discovery:Increase in users navigating between content types (e.g., podcast → song) via shared emotional tags
  • Archive Engagement:Return visits to the emotional archive or “memory space” over time
  • Personalization Effectiveness:Uplift in saves/replays from emotion-driven recommendations vs. baseline recommendations
  • Feedback Loop Health:Volume of emotional tags that users correct, confirm, or adjust, helping improve ML accuracy
  • Emotional Tag Reuse:Frequency with which the same tags are applied across different content types
  • User Sentiment Shifts:Trends in how user emotional states evolve within a session or across sessions (optional advanced ML metric)

All of this gives Spotify a richer signal loop to understand users beyond genres and clicks.

Why it’s different from what Spotify already does?

This isn’t meant to replace what already exists.It’s a layer underneath, a layer of meaning, feeling, and memory.The foundation of this idea builds on what Spotify already does well: mood-based playlists, but takes it a step deeper by shifting from pre-listening prompts to real-time emotional reflection. Today, users might type a prompt like "💔 breakup" and get a playlist curated to that mood. But this proposal imagines emotional input coming during listening, allowing the experience to evolve contextually, not just based on what the user says they want, but how they actually feel.

While current personalization is driven largely by behavioral signals like skips or saves, this approach layers in self-expression like emotions, voice notes, journal-style reflections, which could give the recommendation engine more human-centered data. And when it comes to content memory, Spotify today offers likes and history. This would introduce an entirely new kind of archive: an emotional feed that remembers not just what you played, but how it made you feel, organized with the help of machine learning.

Why I’m thinking about this

I’ve been sketching an emotional framework for a personal project called POV: The Vibe Compass.

It’s not fully built yet, right now it’s honestly sitting in my Notion with scattered thoughts and color-coded tags.

But the idea behind it was to help people navigate content by emotional state:

🔵 Blue when they’re spiraling, 🔴 Red when they’re rebellious, 🟡 Yellow when they need confidence.

Working on that made me realize: emotions aren’t linear, and content doesn’t hit everyone the same way.

So why not let users shape their emotional navigation actively?

Spotify could be more than a discovery engine.

It could be a memory companion, one that not only delivers great content,

but also holds space for how it made you feel when it found you.

Potential Long-Term Impact

  • Turn emotions into metadata that bridges Spotify's verticals (music, podcasts, audiobooks)

  • Creates an emotion-first layer of discovery that’s more human than genre or artist-centric filters

  • Unlocks new surfaces for monetization (e.g., mood-based ad targeting, emotion-aware premium playlists)

  • Improve creator tools that help artists/podcasters tag their own content by emotion, and reach the right audiences accordingly.

    Creators get engagement metrics (like skips, plays, saves), but they don't get insight into how their audience actually feels. This idea could potentially provide emotion-first metadata that both Spotify and creators can use to personalize content strategy, audience outreach, and catalog tagging.

Final thoughts

This is still just a thought exercise, but it’s grounded in the way I build.

I like solving for how it feels, not just what it does.If there’s a world where Spotify helps users tag their own emotional experiences, build a space around them, and discover meaning as much as music, I’d love to help design it :)
