Product Teardown - Notion

A product teardown of Notion based on my real usage, proposing three scalable improvements :block-level analytics, stale page detection, and mood-based themes, to enhance clarity, collaboration, and emotional UX. Built as a self-initiated case study to showcase product thinking.

ℹ️ About the project

This was a video-first product critique submitted as part of a product hiring challenge. The goal was to highlight a tool I use regularly, identify real user friction, and suggest actionable product improvements grounded in behavior, context, and design empathy.

I chose Notion, a product I use every single day for everything from my portfolio and content system to personal task tracking and collaborative workspaces. The prompt was open-ended, but I treated it like a product thinking case study: start from user pain, connect it to product behavior, and propose improvements that scale from individual users to teams.

All of the following suggestions are based on real moments of friction I’ve experienced while using Notion for solo and collaborative work. These are not UI tweaks, they’re workflow-centric, emotion-aware product shifts that could unlock better collaboration and decision-making at scale.

Disclaimer: This was not a commissioned case study or consulting project for Notion. These ideas were proposed independently, as part of a product evaluation exercise.

🚀 Timeline and Context

This case study was structured as a short 2-minute product teardown. I recorded, edited, and narrated a video that included three detailed product improvement proposals. Each idea mapped to a key moment of user friction that I’ve personally felt while using Notion.

Rather than making surface-level suggestions, I focused on changes that would improve clarity, speed up collaboration, and make the product feel more alive and emotionally intelligent, especially in remote team contexts.

1️⃣ Block-Level Analytics

The first improvement I proposed is block-level analytics,a native way to understand not just how many people visited a page, but what they actually engaged with inside the page.

Currently, Notion’s built-in analytics are extremely limited. For my portfolio, I can only see total and unique page views. That data doesn’t help me understand user behavior. Did someone scroll through my case studies? Did they click my LinkedIn? Did they spend time reading or bounce after the intro?

Right now, there’s no way to know, unless I integrate third-party tools like Notionlytics. But those tools aren’t built into the product flow, and they feel detached from the Notion experience.

A better system would include built-in insights at the block level. Think: which section was viewed most, which button was clicked, and where users stopped scrolling. Maybe even lightweight reactions or engagement graphs.

For creators, this feedback loop would be game-changing. You’d instantly know what content resonates, what to amplify, and what to drop.

For remote teams, this means faster alignment. Instead of relying on passive comments or wondering if anyone saw a doc, you’d have visibility into what was reviewed.

Block-level analytics would transform Notion from a static canvas into a feedback-aware workspace, essential for async collaboration and decision-making.

2️⃣Stale Page Detector

The second feature I proposed is what I called a Stale Page Detector or more simply, an automatic way to surface forgotten or outdated content.

In every Notion workspace, clutter accumulates over time. Pages get created, reused, duplicated, and eventually abandoned. My workspace has templates I haven’t touched in months. Some of them I don’t even remember creating.

This makes navigation messy and introduces unnecessary friction. Even for teams, when you’re collaborating across multiple workspaces, the volume of unused pages starts to create noise.

My suggestion is simple: auto-flag inactivity.

If a page hasn’t been viewed or edited in, say, 60 or 90 days, surface a subtle prompt : “This page hasn’t been active in a while. Archive or revive?”

This prompt could be toggled on or off, or adjusted per workspace. It could be layered with metadata like last-viewed-by, last-commented, or linked-to-from-where.

For solo users, it’s a gentle nudge to keep things tidy. For teams, it’s a scalable system for keeping knowledge management relevant, searchable, and aligned with current workflows.

This isn’t just about visual cleanup. It’s about reducing decision fatigue, avoiding duplicated work, and keeping the team focused on what matters now, not what was built three roadmaps ago.

3️⃣Mood-Based Team Themes

The third improvement I proposed is a bit more unconventional, but I think deeply relevant to remote team dynamics: mood-based workspace themes.

The core idea is that productivity isn’t just about features. It’s about how you feel in the space you’re working in. Especially in remote or hybrid teams, the emotional tone of a digital workspace can really affect focus and flow.

So what if Notion had toggleable workspace moods?

Imagine three modes:

  • Energy Mode for brainstorming: bright tones, all blocks visible, reactions turned on.
  • Collab Mode for editing and reviewing: clean neutrals, inline comments emphasized, version history more visible.
  • Focus Mode for deep work: calming blues, sidebar minimized, only key blocks visible.

When I toggle between these modes, the interface subtly shifts, colors change, animations adjust, and certain blocks appear or hide based on what I’ve preset.

This isn’t just aesthetic. For focus mode, I might hide non-essential linked databases. In energy mode, I might see recent edits and prompts to explore.

The experience becomes more immersive, contextual, and emotionally tuned to the task at hand.

Importantly, this wouldn’t break team alignment. Each user could have a personal view, while the workspace still follows a central theme set by the team lead or project manager.

This small shift would help people feel more connected to the workspace and to each other, not just as collaborators, but as humans sharing a creative environment.

Outcomes and Reflections

This wasn’t a live product improvement project, but a rapid critique built to showcase product thinking. What I learned from structuring it this way:

A good feature isn’t just solving a problem, it’s answering why now, for whom, and how often this hurts. Block analytics solve uncertainty. Stale page detection solves distraction. Mood toggles solve emotional disconnection in digital spaces.

These aren’t edge cases. These are deeply felt problems hiding in plain sight — and they scale across solo users, creators, and global teams.

My biggest takeaway was that great product ideas often come from lived experience, not hypotheticals. The features I proposed weren’t imagined in a vacuum. They came from hitting the same friction points over and over until I decided to map them out, name them, and reimagine how they could work better.
